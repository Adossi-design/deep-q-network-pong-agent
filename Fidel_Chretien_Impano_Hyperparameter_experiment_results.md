Every run trained for 500,000 steps with the same seed, and the average reward quoted for each one comes from its last 100 episodes in the log file. Fidel was unavailable during the training week, so Adossi Fred William ran these ten experiments for him on Kaggle with the same train.py and the settings from Fidel's guide. The rewards were recorded while the agent still chose some random moves, so they are a little lower than what each model would score when it plays greedily.

| Experiment | Learning rate | Gamma | Batch size | Epsilon start | Epsilon end | Epsilon decay | Observed behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.0001 | 0.99 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | This is the baseline and it ended with an average reward of -16.61. The reward sat near -20.5 for the first 250,000 steps and then climbed steadily, so the agent had started to return the ball and was still rising when the run stopped. |
| 2 | 0.00025 | 0.99 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | The average reward was -20.95 and it never left the bottom, even though this is the learning rate used in the original DQN paper. The updates were probably too large for this small network and setup, so it kept overshooting instead of settling on a good policy. |
| 3 | 0.00005 | 0.99 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | It finished at -18.67 and only began to rise in the last third of the run. Half the usual learning rate means the weights change more slowly, so it was still behind the baseline when the steps ran out. |
| 4 | 0.001 | 0.99 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | The average reward was -20.95 and it stayed flat at the bottom from start to finish. A learning rate ten times the baseline made each update far too big, so the network never learned anything useful. |
| 5 | 0.0001 | 0.99 | 16 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | It ended at -19.21 and stayed close to the bottom until the last 100,000 steps. A small batch gives a noisier gradient at every update, and that probably made the learning slower and less steady than the baseline. |
| 6 | 0.0001 | 0.99 | 48 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | This was the best run with -15.79, and it was still climbing fast at the end. A somewhat bigger batch averages over more experiences, so the gradient is smoother and the network improves more steadily. |
| 7 | 0.0001 | 0.97 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 100000 | The average reward was -16.28 and it kept rising, a little better than the baseline. Since the gap is under half a point it could easily come from the seed, so I would not read much into the lower gamma. |
| 8 | 0.0001 | 0.99 | 32 | 0.8 | 0.01 | 100000 | It finished at -16.39 and rose at about the same pace as the baseline. Starting epsilon at 0.8 instead of 1.0 removes only a few random moves at the very start, which is why it made so little difference. |
| 9 | 0.0001 | 0.99 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.05 | 100000 | The average reward was -16.39, again close to the baseline, and the reward kept rising. Because epsilon stopped at 0.05, about one move in twenty stayed random, and that cost the score a little but did not stop the learning. |
| 10 | 0.0001 | 0.99 | 32 | 1.0 | 0.01 | 50000 | It ended at -18.05, noticeably worse than the baseline although it did keep rising. Epsilon fell to its low value after only 50,000 steps, so the agent stopped exploring early and probably settled before it had seen enough of the game. |

The best experiment was number 6, where a batch of 48 took the average reward to -15.79 while the baseline reached -16.61. The worst were numbers 2 and 4, which tied at -20.95 and never left the bottom because their learning rates were too high, and number 4 had the larger rate of the two. Across the ten runs the learning rate mattered most, since anything from 0.00025 upwards failed completely and 0.00005 was only slower, while the gamma and epsilon changes made differences small enough to be noise. Each run used a single seed, so the gaps between numbers 1, 6, 7, 8 and 9 are less than a point and should be treated with care, and every run except 2 and 4 was still improving when it stopped. For the final model I would choose the settings of experiment 6, with a learning rate of 0.0001, a gamma of 0.99 and a batch of 48, because it learned fastest and was still gaining. That choice also agrees with Adossi's results, where a larger batch gave the biggest improvement.
